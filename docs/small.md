# Small dimensions

How do you prove that no lattice has a larger $\eta$ than some candidate? In dimensions three and four, one inequality does all the work. It is worth seeing why, because in dimension five it stops working, and the rest of the site is about what replaces it.

## Splitting a flag in two

Take a lattice $L$ in $n$ dimensions, and a sublattice $M$ of rank $k$ inside it. Build a basis of $L$ in two halves. Start with the best basis of $M$, then continue with the best basis of what's left, the projection of $L$ perpendicular to $M$. The potential of this basis splits into a part from $M$ and a part from the projection. Each part is at most the largest value of $\eta$ in its own dimension. Collecting the powers of the volumes gives

$$
\eta(L) \;\le\; \eta_k\,\eta_{n-k}\left(\frac{\det M}{\Delta^{k/n}}\right)^{n/2}.
$$

In the paper: Lemma 3.
{ .paper-ref }

So a lattice with a large $\eta$ cannot have any small sublattice. Every $M$ has to have a large volume compared with the whole lattice.

The simplest case is $k = 1$, with $M$ spanned by a single shortest vector $v$. Then $\det M = |v|^2$, the bracket becomes $\gamma(L) = |v|^2/\Delta^{1/n}$, and

$$
\eta(L) \;\le\; \eta_{n-1}\,\gamma(L)^{n/2} \;\le\; \eta_{n-1}\,\gamma_n^{\,n/2}.
$$

The potential of the whole flag is at most the potential of one dimension fewer, times a power of [Hermite's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_constant). For Hermite's constant the analogous step-down inequality is Mordell's, and Meyer has this one for these constants.

## Dimension three

With $\eta_2 = 2/\sqrt3$ and $\gamma_3 = 2^{1/3}$ the inequality gives only $\eta_3 \le \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\cdot\sqrt2 \approx 1.633$, which is not sharp. The true value, $\eta_3 = 3/2$, attained only by [fcc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-packing_of_equal_spheres) and [bcc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_crystal_system), needs a finer argument. It was proved in the 2007 paper, and independently by Meyer in 2008.

## Dimension four, and D₄

[D₄](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D4_lattice) is the four-dimensional checkerboard: all integer points $(a, b, c, d)$ whose coordinates add up to an even number. Its shortest vectors are the 24 points like $(1, 1, 0, 0)$ and $(1, -1, 0, 0)$, with two coordinates $\pm1$ and the rest zero. Each point of D₄ touches 24 others. It is the densest lattice packing of spheres in four dimensions, and its Voronoi cell is the 24-cell, one of the regular four-dimensional polytopes ([more](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16-cell_honeycomb)).

Now apply the inequality with $n = 4$. Since $\eta_3 = 3/2$ and $\gamma_4 = \sqrt2$,

$$
\eta_4 \;\le\; \eta_3\,\gamma_4^{\,2} \;=\; \tfrac32 \cdot 2 \;=\; 3.
$$

And D₄ has $\eta = 3$. Its best basis has the staircase $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, 1$, so the volumes are $G = (2, 3, 4, 4)$ and

$$
\eta(D_4) = \frac{2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot4}{4^{5/2}} = \frac{96}{32} = 3.
$$

The bound is attained, so $\eta_4 = 3$. This is Meyer's theorem, from his [2008 thesis](https://theses.hal.science/tel-00346870), with the same proof.

The new paper adds uniqueness, which falls out of the same argument. Equality forces $\gamma(L) = \gamma_4$, so $L$ must be a densest lattice in four dimensions, and [Korkine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Korkin) and [Zolotarev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yegor_Ivanovich_Zolotaryov) showed that D₄ is the only one.

In the paper: Theorem 7.
{ .paper-ref }

## Dimension eight, and E₈

[E₈](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E8_lattice) is an exceptionally symmetric eight-dimensional lattice, the densest sphere packing there ([more](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E8_lattice)). Every point touches 240 others. It is isodual: in its usual scaling, $E_8^* = E_8$.

Here the useful split is down the middle, with $k = 4$. Rankin's constant $\gamma_{8,4}$ measures how small a four-dimensional sublattice of an eight-dimensional lattice can be forced to be, and Sawatani, Watanabe and Okuda proved that $\gamma_{8,4} = 4$ in the squared normalisation. So

$$
\eta_8 \;\le\; \eta_4^{\,2}\,\gamma_{8,4}^{\,4} \;=\; 9 \cdot 256 \;=\; 2304,
$$

and E₈ attains it: its staircase is $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, 1, 1, \tfrac34, \tfrac23, \tfrac12$, the volumes are $G = (2, 3, 4, 4, 4, 3, 2, 1)$, and their product is $2304$, with $\Delta = 1$. So $\eta_8 = 2304$.

In the paper: Theorem 8.
{ .paper-ref }

Whether E₈ is the *only* lattice attaining it is open. Chapter 11 shows that it is the only one nearby.

## The root lattices

[A₂](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_lattice), A₃, D₄, [D₅](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D5_lattice) and E₈ belong to one family, the irreducible root lattices. Each is generated by its shortest vectors, the *roots*, all of squared length 2.

- A_n: the points of $\mathbb Z^{n+1}$ whose coordinates add up to zero. A₂ is the hexagonal lattice and A₃ is fcc.
- D_n: the integer points of $\mathbb Z^n$ with even coordinate sum, the checkerboard. D₃ is fcc again.
- [E₆](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E6_lattice), [E₇](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E7_lattice), E₈: three exceptional lattices. E₆ and E₇ sit inside E₈ as sections.

Their structure is the theory of [root systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_system), which Wikipedia covers well. What matters here is that $\eta$ is known exactly for every one of them. The paper proves that the natural *root flag* is Ψ-minimal, because each of its prefix volumes $G_k$ is as small as any rank-$k$ sublattice can be.

| lattice | $\eta$ | Ψ-minimal profile $r_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| A_n | $(n+1)!/(n+1)^{(n+1)/2}$ | $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, \dots, \tfrac{n+1}{n}$ |
| D_n ($n \ge 4$) | $3\cdot 2^{n-4}$ | $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, 1, \dots, 1$ |
| E₆ | $128\sqrt3/9 \approx 24.63$ | $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, 1, 1, \tfrac34$ |
| E₇ | $144$ | $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, 1, 1, \tfrac34, \tfrac23$ |
| E₈ | $2304$ | $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43, 1, 1, \tfrac34, \tfrac23, \tfrac12$ |

In the paper: Theorem 5 and Table 2.

## A flag that is extremal in every rank

For D₄, D₅, E₆, E₇ and E₈, something stronger is true. Scale each lattice so that its volume is 1. Then the volume $G_k$ of *every* step of the root flag is as small as Hermite's bound allows for a $k$-dimensional sublattice. The shortest vector is as long as possible, the densest plane is as sparse as possible, and so on all the way up.

The number this gives in dimension $m$ is

$$
H_m = \frac{\gamma_m^{\,m(m-1)/2}}{\prod_{k<m}\gamma_k^{\,k}},
$$

and $H_3, \dots, H_8 = \tfrac32,\ 3,\ 6,\ \tfrac{128\sqrt3}{9},\ 144,\ 2304$, exactly the values of $\eta$ for A₃, D₄, D₅, E₆, E₇ and E₈. Every lattice has $\eta \le \eta_m$, and the critical lattice always has $\eta \ge H_m$, so $\eta_m \ge H_m$.

In the paper: §4.3.
{ .paper-ref }

The pattern suggests a guess: $\eta_m = H_m$ for every $m \le 8$, attained by the densest lattice. It is now proved for $m \le 5$ and $m = 8$, and conjectured for 6 and 7.

In dimension five, though, the splitting inequality can't reach the guess. The $k = 1$ split gives $\eta_5 \le \eta_4\,\gamma_5^{5/2} = 3\cdot 8^{1/2} \approx 8.49$, and the best split, with the Bergé–Martinet refinement, gives $3\cdot2^{5/4} \approx 7.135$. Both are well above 6. Something else is needed. The next chapter says what the answer looks like, and chapter 6 says why the natural attack nearly works.
